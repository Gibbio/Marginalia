# Opening

The room was quiet, but not still: from the corridor came a draft, from the window a slanted light, from the table the faint reflection of a half-empty cup. Mark paused for a moment, listened, then said softly: "Let's hear how this voice actually sounds." No one replied; and yet, in that small emptiness, the sentence already seemed to carry weight, cadence — almost a presence.

He walked slowly toward the bookshelf, the way people do when they aren't just looking for an object, but for a tone, a rhythm, something to hold on to. He pulled a notebook from the row, opened it, read two lines, and smiled: not at the content, but at the punctuation. There were short commas, sharp full stops, an abrupt question, and even an aside in parentheses (perhaps too long, but useful for testing whether the voice keeps its breath). "Good," he murmured. "Here we'll know right away whether the result sounds natural or not."

# Corridor

The corridor of the building, at that hour, had a peculiar echo: every footstep seemed to come back a fraction of a second late, as if the walls reflected not only the sound but also the doubt. One, two, three steps; then a pause. Another pause, even longer. And finally a clipped sentence: "If the voice rushes, you can hear it. If it drags, you can hear it too."

On the landing sat a forgotten plant, almost dry, with two leaves folded downward. It wasn't an important detail, and that's exactly why it worked well: synthetic voices often stumble over minor things — the everyday words that ought to carry no drama at all. Leaves, vase, water, shadow, dust. Simple vocabulary; harsh judgement.

# Dialogue

"Did you try the previous chapter already?" Laura asked, stepping in without knocking.

"Yes," Mark answered. "The voice is clear, but I want to see how it handles a change of intent: a question, a statement, an ironic line — and then back to a neutral tone."

"Then read her this passage," she said. "Not the most elegant one: the most human one."

Mark nodded, found the page, and began: "Not everything has to impress on the first pass; sometimes it's enough for a voice to stay present, sentence after sentence, without turning theatrical, without disappearing, without drawing too much attention." He stopped, tilted his head, then added, almost laughing: "And now let's see the exclamation mark!"

"Right," said Laura. "Because reading well is one thing. Sounding believable is another."

# List

For a quick test, you only need a handful of things:

First: a short sentence, to gauge the attack.

Second: a long sentence, with subordinate clauses, with a sudden sidestep, with a rhythm change that forces the voice to choose where to breathe and where to push through.

Third: a direct question. "Does it really work?" If the answer, at least to the ear, sounds plausible, the model has cleared an important hurdle.

Fourth: a more narrative fragment, almost slow, with common words and a few slightly more precise ones; nothing artificial, nothing emphatic, but enough material to reveal flaws.

Fifth: a short closing. Precise. Memorable.

# Closing

In the end, the criterion was simple: don't look for a perfect voice, look for a steady one. A voice that doesn't trip over commas, that doesn't butcher an interrogative clause, that doesn't turn every pause into a suspicion or every full stop into a hammer blow. A voice that can read a whole text without wearing down the listener.

Mark closed the notebook, looked out the window, and said calmly: "If after five minutes I don't feel like interrupting it, we're on the right track." Then he hit enter, listened to the first second of audio, and stayed silent. That was the moment of the real test.
